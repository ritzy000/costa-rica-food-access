Economic Definition

Defining Home Staples (Canasta Básica Alimentaria)

Official standard monitored by MEIC and INEC in Costa Rica.

In Costa Rica, home staples are formalized under the Canasta Básica Alimentaria (CBA). This basket represents the minimum essential food items required to meet the daily caloric and nutritional needs of a average Costa Rican household, serving as a baseline for economic policy and food security monitoring.

Grains & Pulses

White rice, black/red beans, corn tortillas, and fortified wheat flour.

Fresh Produce

Bananas, plantains, potatoes, tomatoes, onions, cassava (yuca), and carrots.

Dairy & Eggs

Fluid milk, fresh white cheese (Queso Turrialba), butter/natilla, and chicken eggs.

Proteins & Fats

Chicken cuts, ground beef, canned tuna, vegetable oils, and basic condiments.

Historical Evolution

History of Food Production & Access in Costa Rica

From pre-colonial indigenous agriculture to multinational export empires and modern supermarket chains.

1
Pre-Colonial – 1800s

Indigenous Milpas & Subsistence Farming

Early agriculture relied on diverse agro-ecosystems based on maize, cassava, beans, squash, and tropical fruits. Food security was localized, community-managed, and closely tied to soil cycles and seasonal rainfall.

2
Late 1800s – Mid 1900s

The Coffee & Banana Export Boom

Costa Rica integrated into global trade via coffee high-altitude plantations and lowland banana enclaves (led by entities like the United Fruit Company). While driving infrastructure and economic growth, it reshaped rural land ownership and diverted prime land from staple crops toward export commodities.

3
1970s – 1990s

State Support & Institutionalization of Ferias

To protect small farmers from predatory middlemen (*intermediarios*), the Costa Rican government established official guidelines for the Feria del Agricultor system. This empowered local growers to sell directly to consumers in town squares every weekend.

4
2000s – Present

Modern Supermarket Consolidation & Global Supply Chains

Rapid expansion of corporate supermarket chains reshaped retail access. Today, neighborhood pulperías, weekly ferias, and large retail chains coexist, while global trade dynamics mean staple items like wheat and yellow corn rely heavily on imports.

Retail Ecosystem

Types of Grocery Stores in Costa Rica

Food access in Costa Rica spans diverse market formats, each serving distinct socio-economic roles.

Pulpería

Small corner store deeply tied to local neighborhoods. Offers quick essentials, snacks, and small packaged goods.

Mini-Súper

Mid-sized self-service store with broader staple selection, cold dairy, and basic cleaning supplies.

Supermercado

Large supermarket chains (e.g., Walmart, Masxmenos, Auto Mercado) with high product variety and imports.

Feria del Agricultor

Weekly outdoor farmers' market directly linking regional growers with urban households.

Wholesale (CENADA)

National distribution hub supplying restaurants, institutions, pulperías, and regional markets.

Supply Chain Transparency

Where Do Grocery Stores Source Their Products?

Food sourcing in Costa Rica is divided between domestic smallholder cooperatives, national distribution networks, and global agro-industrial conglomerates.

Local Cooperatives & Smallholders

Regions like Zarcero, Cartago, and San Carlos supply fresh vegetables directly to weekly ferias and independent vegetable stands (*ventanitas*).

CENADA Central Wholesale Market

The national wholesale market in Barreal de Heredia acts as the main hub where pulperías and restaurants buy wholesale produce daily.

Multinational Agro-Corporations (e.g. Chiquita)

Global banana and pineapple exporters (like Chiquita Brands International and Del Monte) manage large-scale plantations in the Atlantic and Pacific lowlands. While primarily focused on export markets in Europe and North America, lower-grade or surplus fruits feed into domestic supermarket chains.

Community Markets

Guide to Farmer's Markets (Ferias del Agricultor)

Visiting a local feria is the most affordable and sustainable way to purchase farm-fresh produce in Costa Rica.

Best Time to Visit

Saturdays and Sundays early morning (6:00 AM – 9:00 AM) for the best produce selection and freshest organic items.

Payment & Mobile App

Bring cash in colones or use SINPE Móvil. Most local farmers accept quick instant mobile transfers.

Reusable Bags

Bring sturdy canvas bags (*mecha*) or cloth sacks to minimize single-use plastic bag usage at stalls.

Benefits & Reasons to Buy Local Produce

Nutritional & Freshness

Produce harvested at peak ripeness within 24–48 hours retains higher vitamin, antioxidant, and mineral levels compared to long-haul cold storage produce.

Direct Local Economy

Up to 85–90% of your spend goes directly to the farming family, cutting out corporate intermediary margins and supporting smallholder livelihood.

Agrobiodiversity

Support traditional Costa Rican heirloom crops (e.g., *pejibaye*, *mamón chino*, *cas*, *pepino dulce*) that aren't stocked in standardized supermarket aisles.

Soil & Community Action

Information on Compost & Local Initiatives

How households and municipalities in San José / Zapote manage organic waste.

Home & Neighborhood Composting Guide

Layer green nitrogen-rich material (fruit peels, coffee grounds, vegetable trimmings) with dry carbon-rich material (dry leaves, shredded cardboard, sawdust) in a 1:2 ratio.

Ideal for urban patios and apartment bokashi bins.

Municipal Initiatives in San José / Zapote

The Municipality of San José (MSJ) and local district committees promote community organic collection points near neighborhood ferias to produce organic soil conditioner for public parks.
